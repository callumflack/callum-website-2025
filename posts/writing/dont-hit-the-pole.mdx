Kristof Kieslowski's “Three Colours: Blue” begins with an open landscape with a car cutting through it. Then we see the aftermath where the car has crashed into the only tree across the span of this open landscape. It always seemed to me that the driver got himself into a pickle then told himself “don’t hit the tree” and the more he focused on that, the more his actions led to the opposite: hitting the tree.

This is called [Target Fixation](https://grokipedia.com/page/Target_fixation): when attention locks onto an object so tightly that action begins bending toward it. I’m interested in it less as a driving phenomenon than as a clue: language can say “not,” but the body still has to attend to an object.

This is easiest to see by following the same pattern through a few scales: the body, the sentence, the model, and the crowd.

## The body

Instead of expressing a clear and simple instruction, “don’t hit the pole” can quickly become a Boolean mixup of two simultaneous mental states that can lead to the opposite of the instruction, especially when we are not in our “right mind”.

When we explicitly set out to do something, we create a doing _and_ a not-doing that the doing must surpass. The challenge of the language-based logic that hasn’t been formally understood within oneself twists into a tension between doing and not-doing. And the not-do becomes the do.

Motorcycle training gives the better instruction: [“don’t look where you don’t want to go”](https://x.com/nicholaspaul26/status/1699325230605635990). The body follows the line of attention. If you stare at the hazard, you begin steering toward it. So look at the line out.

Parenting children shows the same problem. Instructing with “don’t” requires the child to perform a kind of abstraction they may not yet have, so “don’t” can reinforce the behaviour you're asking them not to do. Instead, give the child's body something to do: hold it with two hands, walk slowly, put your hand here, look this way. The intended action has to become more available than the avoided one.

## The sentence

Language requires us to construct the very thing we're trying to avoid. To process “don't think of a pink elephant” we must first think of “a pink elephant” and then negate it. Thus, the damage is done. Our embodied attentional system—the thing that does—does not have a Boolean _not_ operator, it only acts in relation to physical objects. Beneath language lies an orientation system that only understands positive instruction: “look”, “move”, “hold”.

Target Fixation seems to be created out of the tension between our linguistic-logical system and our embodied attention system. [Cormac McCarthy](https://nautil.us/cormac-mccarthy-returns-to-the-kekul-problem-236896/) ventures this is because “the unconscious mind is a biologically determined system that predates language, which is not inherently biological.”

Another one is JavaScript Boolean coercion, for example `!!isActive`. It takes a while to see this intuitively without second guessing. That is, I am forced to check my glance to be sure the syntax is doing what I want, which in this case is presented as “what I don’t don’t want”. JavaScript Boolean coercion, like every other invented code syntax, works by requiring pure symbolism, but every time I read it, I must traverse between my intention (which is at my fingertips in this situation) and the invented language’s execution protocol. Ceci n'est pas une pipe! (Indeed, what language is not invented?)

Here’s a few idioms that play with forms of Target Fixation and its negative inversion:

- “The more you aim at it and make it a target, the more you're going to miss it.” — Victor Frankl
- “What you think you are, made you stuck” — True Detective, Season 3

## The sentence 2

Language makes negation look simple. “Don’t think of a pink elephant” seems to contain a command and its cancellation in the same sentence. But to understand the sentence, you must first construct the pink elephant. Only then can you negate it.

This is the problem: negation does not prevent representation. It requires representation. The object enters the room before language tells it to leave.
Our embodied attentional system does not seem to have the same clean Boolean operator. The sentence can say not, but the body still orients by “look”, “move”, “hold”, “avoid”, “reach”.

Cormac McCarthy gestures at this split when he argues that the unconscious predates language. Language can produce formal negation, but the older system underneath acts in relation to objects and affordances.

JavaScript Boolean coercion gives a small technical version of the same problem. !!isActive is readable, but it asks me to compute intention through symbolic inversion: not-not-active. The code is precise, but my glance still has to translate it.

This is why negation is never just absence. It is a way of summoning the thing under the sign of refusal.

## The model

We can see this language-comprehension trap is widely applicable and it relates to some kind of tension between the abstractions of language and enacting the instructions of language with one’s body. So it is not surprising when we also find Target Fixation within Large Language Models. Even though they don’t have bodies, they have the _corpus_ of human text, within which is recorded this tension:

> [The Waluigi Effect](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/D7PumeYTDPfBTp3i7/the-waluigi-effect-mega-post): After you train an LLM to satisfy a desirable property P, then it's easier to elicit the chatbot into satisfying the exact opposite of property P.

## The crowd

What happens when Target Fixation migrates from the individual to an unconscious society-wide drive? Here’s a few more idioms to ponder:

- “Problems expand to occupy the anxiety available” — [Venkat Rao](https://x.com/vgr/status/1053858861008871425?s=21)
- [“Don’t immanentize the eschaton”](https://x.com/Artofhunger75/status/1878325910820077734)
- “Prediction markets are hyperstitious machines” — [Christian](https://x.com/curious_vii/status/1951340627339129050)
- “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” — Goodhart’s Law
- “Tasks expand to fill the time available” — Parkinson’s Law

Populations, societies and markets where the vibe incentivizes the outcome can easily veer into collective forms of Target Fixation. The negative axiom “don’t hit the pole!” is _dis_-attended to and dissolves through attention entropy—ensloppification of vigilance, if you will—into “nothing ever happens” vibes. Recursive loops of “waiting for something to happen” create a cosmic unconscious pile-up at the door of the “something happening” queue. The “nothing ever happens” complaint in real terms is the Giant Slot Machine In The Sky’s devilish last hope: [“I can’t help it, I secretly want to hit the pole”](https://x.com/agustinlebron3/status/1946114875899486383?s=12). Monitoring the Situation seems to move oneself into a continuous state of Global Abstraction, obviously mediated by the [Glass Gestell](https://thelittoralline.substack.com/p/ll15). Betting on it starts to feel a little bit like, [uh ahem](https://x.com/PolymarketSport/status/1953143960391626775), immanentising the eschaton. What is [hyperstition](https://cityofplay.substack.com/p/understanding-hyperstition) but the desire for self-fulfilling prophecies by role-playing angels and demons? If you've infected yourself with Target Fixation, then hyperstition is a bit like [Bernard Black torturing Manny](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0526553/?ref_=ttep_ep_2): the joke is coming but it’s _not not not_ funny:

> Suddenly, Manny shrieks in pain, before his phone rings. After he takes the call, Bernard asks him why he shrieked before the phone rang. Manny tells him that he used to use his phone a lot which had a side effect on him so now, just before his phone rings, he gets a sharp pain in his head. Bernard starts torturing Manny by calling his phone continuously, much to his amusement.

## The fix

Target Fixation at the individual and group levels is a _dis_-ease of language-based logic that creates distorted behaviours and unintended consequences. Yet for every derivative I've listed here—focal attention, task time-spaces, measures, stuckness, ensloppification, hyperstition, etc—each is a stumbling block that can evolve toward good or bad outcomes. The fix for each fixation is very simple: “don't look where you don't want to go”. Our motorcycle training idiom becomes a sort of judo move on the negation by inverting the inversions, and by requiring the “second glance” to “compute the boolean”, we regain our vigilance. Once we “snap out of it”, we can further compress it by removing the abstract “do not” booleans, giving ourselves the cleanest of positive instructions: [“focus on more of what you want”](https://x.com/connerdelights/status/1925234602286932171).
