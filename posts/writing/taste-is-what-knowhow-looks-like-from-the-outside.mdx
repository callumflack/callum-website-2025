Calling someone tasteful tells you very little about how they learned to make things.

In [The matter of taste](/the-matter-of-taste), written in 2018, I described taste as the ability to see and act within a problem space. Practice compresses earlier decisions into intuitions. You can get to the heart of a problem because you have already worked through some of what surrounds it.

In that essay I tried to make taste name this capacity. I now think the word too easily redirects our attention. We see the work, admire its apparent ease, and attribute a quality to the person. The attempts, mistakes and corrections disappear into the compliment.

Taste is what knowhow looks like from the outside.

Consider the instruction to give a product better taste. You might collect references, choose a quieter typeface, simplify the palette and add more space. These can all be good decisions. But their goodness depends on what they do in the thing you are making. More space can clarify a relationship or break it. A quieter interface can become harder to read. Consistency can conceal a difference the user needs to understand.

The work starts when the reference stops answering.

## What the eye has learned

I [wrote recently](/you-cant-design-a-ui-without-designing-the-code) about a data-connector interface at Vana. Its production embed, demo lab and error states were rendered separately. Each had a different idea of the user's state. Changes could leave the three versions disagreeing about the same experience.

The correction was to make the real route the renderer and make state canonical. The lab then showed the product itself. That made the interface possible to reason about and design coherently.

You could call the resulting coherence good taste. But that description leaves out the decision that produced it. Improving the interface required recognising that its visible inconsistencies belonged to the structure underneath. The correction had to reach that structure.

This is what I mean by knowhow: being able to find a consequential difference and do something with it. The decision may arrive as a feeling before you can explain it. Something is off. You stay with it, make a change, and see whether the work improves. Over time, you learn which feelings are worth following.

Those feelings can be wrong. Working in the medium gives them somewhere to be corrected.

## The signs travel easily

Calling something tasteful also identifies what a particular scene recognises and rewards. Knowing the references helps you belong. You may be able to explain why an object is admired without knowing how to make the next consequential decision in it. Recognition and operative capacity can reinforce each other. They can also come apart.

AI makes it easier to produce something bearing the visible signs of considered work. A familiar layout, restrained typography, convincing prose. The result can be useful. It can also invite confidence before the conditions of use have been examined.

Recognising the references is one ability. Making a decision when those references conflict is another. Neither the presence of fashionable details nor their absence settles whether the thing works.

The same distinction applies to the person directing the model. Rejecting an output because it feels wrong begins a design decision. The decision still has to go somewhere. What matters here? What would you change? Does the result survive that change when someone uses it?

Calling the human contribution "taste" can make this responsibility sound pleasantly effortless. It asks for the judgment while leaving its formation unexplained.

An agent can participate in the correcting process. It can implement alternatives, expose states, run checks, and carry an accepted decision into shared code. What matters is whether the process remains answerable to the thing being made. A convincing explanation of the change is only one part of that.

## Keep the practice

People have preferences, attractions and aversions before they acquire expertise. We can enjoy things we could never make. Taste has a place in describing that experience. My objection is to using the word as a complete explanation of someone's capacity to make good work.

Knowhow directs attention back to that capacity. It asks what the person has worked on, what has resisted them, and which distinctions they have learned to notice. It also makes learning seem possible. A correction can be watched, attempted, challenged and understood.

The resistance has to matter to the work. Fighting a broken tool can consume an afternoon without improving your judgment. Discovering that a beautiful interface becomes confusing with real content gives you something to work on. The useful difficulty makes a difference visible and gives the next attempt a better chance.

If you want to learn from someone's taste, look for the corrections that made it possible.
